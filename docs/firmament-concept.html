Firmament

A god game of indirect control. Five islands, five tribes, each grown from one man and one woman. You command the weather and the plague. They marry, work, feud, fight and build boats, and the long test is whether they ever reach the Far Shore.

Hybrid civilization

A crowd of numbers, and a few real people

Simulating every person in a 10,000-strong civilization for a thousand years is slow and hard to tune. Simulating only numbers loses the stories. Firmament does both, at three levels of detail.

Stored · always simulated

The crowd

Each village is one record: population, food, jobs, tech, belief and memory. This is what the simulation ticks every season.

  • Cheap to run at 100× speed
  • Easy to balance in data tables
  • Drives every decision a village makes
Drawn · only when zoomed in

The sample

When you zoom into a village, the game draws people from its numbers. If 30% of Aldmouth fishes, 30% of the figures walk to the boats.

  • Never saved, rebuilt on demand
  • Shows the record honestly
  • Costs nothing when you zoom out
Stored · individual agents

The named few

A handful of people are real agents with a name, age, goal, belief and memory. They are the faces of history: the raft-binder, the navigator, the captain.

  • About 3 per village, 30 in the world
  • Their choices can tip a village
  • They write the chronicle

When someone is promoted to a named agent

  • They are the first to do something: first crossing, first sail, first shrine.
  • The story needs a role filled: navigator, priest, founder, rebel.
  • They survive an event the village will remember.

What named agents can change

  • Speed up a tech: a navigator makes star navigation possible.
  • Shift belief: a priest teaches the village how to read your weather.
  • When they die they become a memory and a line in the chronicle.
Characters · the Tailfolk

One look, five bloodlines

Every islander shares one silhouette: a wide straw hat, a big fluffy two-tone tail, wrist wraps, a cloth sash, bare feet and a bare chest for men. Chunky outlines and flat cel shading keep them readable when they are 12 pixels tall at village zoom.

Hat

The island mark

Each tribe paints its own glyph on the hat brim: mountain, wave, tree, gull, sun. You can tell where someone is from at a glance.

Tail

The bloodline

Tail color is inherited. Children of two islands get a blended, banded tail, so you can watch families mix across the sea.

Tools and sash

The job

What they carry and the color of their sash show their work: a hoe, a net-spear, a mallet, a herb pouch, a sky staff.

Year 0 · one founding pair on each island

Every tribe starts with exactly two people. They are generalists who gather, build and raise children together. Jobs appear as the tribe grows.

Bloodline · tails mix when islands marry
From two people to a nation · illustrative model

The first 40 years are the danger zone. If one founder dies before children are grown, the tribe can end in a single season. Your gentlest weather matters most here.

Society · jobs, marriage, war

Everyone works toward the journey

Each settlement's record holds job shares. Every season the leader moves people toward the biggest need: more farmers in a lean year, more warriors under threat, more boatwrights when the sea is calling.

2 to 15 people

Everyone gathers and builds. The founders are leader, doctor and priest at once.

15 to 60

1,500 and up

Chiefdom

Several villages under one chief. Can unite an island, or start wars across the sea.

Marriage and repopulation

  1. PairingAdults pair from age 16. Couples have a child every 2 to 4 years while food stores stay above half.
  2. Kinship limitAfter three generations everyone on a small island is related. Couples must marry out, so tribes seek other islands.
  3. Marriage alliancesA marriage between two tribes cuts the chance of war between them for a generation and shares a technology.
  4. SplittingWhen a village grows past its land, a young family leaves to found a new settlement on the same island.

What pushes them to fight

  1. ScarcityYour drought empties the granary; the next village over still has grain.
  2. BlameA plague arrived on their boats. The sickness is remembered as theirs.
  3. CrowdingTwo growing villages want the same river valley.
  4. AmbitionA named leader wants to unite the island, or a rival heir splits the village.

Toward peace

  1. StrangersThey know nothing about each other. First contact is tense.
  2. Gift exchangeA go-between learns a few words. Fish for obsidian.
  3. Trade routeRegular canoes. Ideas and technologies spread.
  4. Marriage allianceFamilies are joined. Tails start to mix.
  5. FederationIslands share a council and defend each other.

Toward war

  1. GrudgeAn insult, a theft or a plague goes into memory.
  2. RaidYoung warriors steal food or burn a field.
  3. ArmingBoth sides make weapons and raise defences. Builders leave the fields.
  4. WarBattles cost lives on both sides. Villages can be burned or captured.
  5. EndingTruce, conquest, exile to another island, or a marriage that ends the feud.

Every warrior is a person not farming. Arming costs food, so a tribe that arms too early can starve, and one that arms too late can burn.

God power · plague

Sickness travels on every boat

The plague spreads only through contact: shared paths on an island, trade canoes, marriages and wars. An isolated village is safe. A well-connected one can lose everyone. Pick a settlement, choose a strain and release it.

The Far Shore test

Failure is how they learn

A lost boat raises the village's fear of the sea, but it also pushes hull and navigation research forward. Push them too hard and they stop sailing for a generation. Never test them and they never need to improve.

The Far Shore can also be found by accident: a fishing canoe blown east by a storm you sent. If the crew survives and comes home, the whole archipelago knows the Far Shore exists.

Ideas I would add

Making your five features work together

Your features share one thread: contact. Boats carry brides, trade, plague and war at the same time. These additions make that tension the heart of the game. The three outlined in amber are the ones I would build first.

Recommended · ties pairs to voyages

Must marry out

One founding pair means everyone is cousins by the third generation. The tribe has to find partners on another island or slowly weaken. This gives them a reason to cross the sea long before they need land.

Recommended · the core tension

The contact paradox

The same canoe that brings a bride and a new tool can bring plague or a war party. Isolation keeps them safe but stuck. Contact makes them grow and puts them at risk. Your plague power sits right on that line.

Recommended · gentle power

Signs and omens

A cheap, harmless power: a comet, a double rainbow, a red moon. The sky-speaker interprets it, and you steer belief without killing anyone. Signs can call for peace, a voyage or a new leader.

Bloodlines

Traits travel with tails

Each founding pair has a small trait bias: patient, bold, curious, wary. Children inherit a mix. Marrying into a bold bloodline makes a cautious tribe more likely to sail.

Trade and war

One unique resource per island

Hearth has grain, Kelp Isle has fish, Thornholm has hardwood, Gull Rock has feathers and eggs, the Far Shore has obsidian. Every island needs something it lacks. That drives trade, and greed drives war.

Memory

Grudges and debts

Tribes remember who brought sickness, who burned a field and who sent food in a famine. Old debts make alliances, old grudges start wars generations later.

Leadership

Succession crises

When a leader dies, two heirs may claim the role. The village can split in two and found a rival settlement, so civil war grows out of the story with no extra rules.

First contact

Languages drift apart

Isolated tribes slowly develop their own words. First contact needs a go-between, a named Speaker. Without one, a misunderstanding can turn a gift exchange into a raid.

Core loop · indirect control

One turn of the world

You never give orders. The game runs in real time, but the simulation thinks in seasons, and each season runs the same four beats.

  1. 1

    Read the world

    Zoom from the whole archipelago down to one fisher. Overlays show rain, food, belief and who is planning a voyage.

  2. 2

    Shape the sky

    Spend divine energy on rain, wind, storm or drought. Paint it with a brush or drag a storm track.

  3. 3

    They adapt

    Villages harvest, starve, build boats or abandon the coast. Named people make their own choices inside that.

  4. 4

    History remembers

    The event goes into the chronicle and into belief. Faith refills your energy, so how they see you matters.

Development framework

How the game gets built

A simulation game lives or dies on whether the simulation can be trusted, tested and tuned. These four rules come before any feature work.

Rule A

Simulation owns the truth

All game state lives in one plain data object. The renderer only reads it and never changes it.

Rule B

Systems are pure steps

Each system takes state, events and a random source and returns new state. Every one can be unit-tested alone.

Rule C

Seeds replay everything

One seed plus a list of player actions rebuilds any run exactly. A bug report becomes a replay file.

Rule D

Detail on demand

Terrain detail and crowds are generated only for what is on screen. Zooming never changes the simulation.

Each layer reads only from the layers below it. The simulation runs in a Web Worker and posts a snapshot after each tick. The crowd sampler on the main thread turns village records into visible people, so the worker never stores them.

sim/tick.ts · the whole game in one function
export function tick(w: World, acts: Action[], rng: Rng): World {
  w = applyPowers(w, acts);  // player edits sky + ground
  w = climate(w, rng);        // season, wind, weather cells
  w = terrain(w);             // floods, silt, erosion
  w = ecology(w);             // soil, crops, forests, fish
  w = society(w, rng);        // villages: growth, hunger
  w = agents(w, rng);         // named people: goals, choices
  w = voyages(w, rng);        // boats at sea, discovery
  w = culture(w);             // tech, belief, memory
  w.chronicle.push(...narrate(w.events));
  return w;
}

Visual art concept

Living Atlas

The world looks like a hand-tinted survey map that has started to breathe. Weather is ink wash drifting over the paper, and people are the only warm light on it.

Readable from orbit

Contour bands, hillshade and sea depth make the island ladder obvious. You can see the shallow reef to Kelp Isle and the deep water before the Far Shore.

Detail grows as you zoom

Lights become roofs and fields, then trees and walking people. Clouds thin out as you go below them, so the ground is always readable.

People are light

Villages, figures and boats carry the warm colors. Named people get an ember ring so they stand out from the crowd.

Type

An engraved serif for place names, chronicle text and headings, so the world reads like a carved record. A clean sans for the interface, and a monospace for dates and numbers.

Sound

Low drones for the sky that change with the weather. Surf that grows louder as you zoom to the coast. Voices and tools at village zoom. Disasters arrive with silence first.

Motion

The land never animates on its own. Weather drifts, boats sail, people walk. Change on the ground happens over seasons, so it feels earned.
